There is a scene in Michael Mann's *Collateral* that should have ruined the city for everyone who watched it. A hitman, bored and almost kind, tells a cab driver about a man who died on a subway train in Los Angeles. He did not fall. He did not scream. He sat down, died, and kept riding. Hours passed. People got on. People got off. Nobody noticed.

We laughed in the cinema because it was easier than the alternative, which was looking at the person next to us.

## The crowd is the perfect hiding place

We were told that crowds keep us safe. More eyes, more witnesses, more help. The opposite is true. In a crowd every person assumes somebody else has already seen it, already called it in, already cared. A thousand eyes become zero. The more of us there are, the more invisible each of us becomes.

That man on the train was not killed by his heart. He was buried by our attention, which was busy with a screen, a playlist, a deadline, the back of a stranger's head.

> Most people die without seeing the one. The one person, the one chance, the one warning that was sitting right beside them.

## You are riding the same train

Ask yourself what you walked past today. The neighbour whose lights have not come on in a week. The friend who stopped joking. The bill that quietly changed. The decision about your street, your school, your rent, made in a room you did not know existed, announced in a notice nobody read.

None of it shouted. That is the trick. The things that end us rarely shout. They sit very still and ride the train with us until we arrive somewhere we never chose.

I am not here to comfort you. Comfort is what put you to sleep. I am here to tap you on the shoulder, in a carriage full of people, and ask the question nobody else will.

<span class="key">Look up. Who is sitting next to you?</span>
